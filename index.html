      About this project

      Mapping the Affective Geographies of Covid-19: A Kaleidoscopic Online Archive

      This project is a collaborative, artistic and experimental science communication project that emerged from the context of the Moreppar-research on the Mobility Regimes of Pandemic Preparedness and Response in the Case of Covid-19, an international research cooperation on the affective dimensions of Covid-19 in the Democratic Republic Kongo, Germany, South Africa and South Korea. The involved institutions are the Yonsei University in Seoul, South Korea, the Witwatersrand University in Johannesburg, South Africa, the Pole Institute in Goma, Democratic Republic Congo, the Free University in Berlin, Germany and the Bernhard-Nocht-Institute for Tropical Medicine in Hamburg, Germany.

      It is an interactive memory project that sits at the intersection of collection, archive, and exhibition, and aims to give insight into the felt experience of public health emergencies, more specifically the Covid-19 pandemic. The material on this website mostly stems from ethnographic research conducted in the framework of the MoRePPaR-research and collects experiences and voices from our interlocutors mixed with photos, videos and sounds from their life worlds. All materials have been collected and curated from 2022 onwards, as the pandemic had already mostly subsided – and yet is lingering in memories, emotions and spaces people inhabit.

      Affective Atmospheres

      The development of this project has been shaped by a phenomenological consideration: our experience and perception of the world are understood as co-constituted by atmospheres, affective “tunings” of bodies and space. It reads public health emergencies and the mobility regimes that govern them as profoundly spatial situations that people inhabit and experience feelingly through the spaces they encounter them in – their homes, their workplaces, their life worlds. And it proposes that the e/merging Viral Atmospheres shape people’s perception of these situations and their interaction with and memories of them.

      “Atmospheres” colloquially refer to what can be understood as a certain mood or “tune” of a space and has been theorized about by different philosophers, most prominently in the tradition of phenomenology, by Hermann Schmitz, Gernot Böhme and Tonino Griffero. Their theories, including the writings of Frantz Fanon on atmospheres, have guided the curation, conceptual and aesthetic development of this project.

      The theory on atmospheres examines the relation of feelings and space.

      Imagine the feeling of being inside a church, walking through an empty street during a snowy winter night or sitting in a full football stadium during a match. Imagine the bright light and the people around you cheering and singing as the match unfolds in the distant middle of the stadium. The way we are grasped by and co-create the atmosphere emerging from the cheering masses, the light, the tension in the voice of the commentators, or the quietness of the snow in the golden light of the street lamps are easy to understand examples of the phenomenon these philosophers have theorized about.

      The theory approaches the relationship between feelings and space from two perspectives. It looks both at how feelings extend beyond the individual body into space and how, through that, feelings can become collectively shared, and at how space is affectively “tuned” by the situational encounter of material and non-material beings (human and non-human)—an affectively tuned space that we perceive through our felt-body, the entity Schmitz understands as the entity we feel internal bodily movement with—emotions like anxiety as well as physical sensations like exhaustion. This transsubjective connection between and merging of space and feelings, of “inner and outer world” (Riedel), is understood as being mediated through and created by atmospheres. Atmospheres here are often understood as all-pervading (Griffero, everything is atmospherical) and as a fundamental mode of being-in-the-world (Heidegger), and perceiving it that turns our interaction with the world into “meaningful situations” (Riedel, Schmitz, Griffero). In the sense that they shape our perception, atmospheres are understood to structure our field of action (Collective Volume Trigg, Griffero, Böhme). While they do not affect everyone in the same way, nevertheless they involve and affect all bodies that encounter and become enmeshed in them.

      In this project, we focus on “viral” atmospheres—we are interested in how the Covid-19 pandemic was felt through the atmospheres that tinted people’s experiences thereof. We argue that the distinctly spatial dimensions of the pandemic and the mobility regimes that governed it profoundly shaped people’s affective experience of this public health emergency and their memories thereof in multiple ways. As many of the stories in this collection reveal, people’s living environments shaped the ways public health regimes during the pandemic unfolded and affected lives, routines and affective experience of it. At the same time, mobility regimes and other pandemic regulations and practices in many cases changed how people were able to inhabit previously familiar spaces. Pandemic regimes reconfigured how spaces were felt and navigated, and infused them with new meaning, changing how inner and outer worlds met and merged atmospherically.

      Our focus is guided by the aim of giving an account of how people remember the pandemic emotionally, understanding these memories as lingering traces of pandemic atmospheres – proposing that these accounts hold a subjective kind of knowledge about the lived realities of public health emergencies that can help us understand how people relate to and act within them, as well as how these past realities continue to shape people’s affective worlds even as the pandemic situations have dissolved.

      What remains of the viral atmospheres of the pandemic?

      As Griffero argues, in order to research atmospheres, one must immerse oneself in them; subjective experience is detrimental to studying the touching and merging of inner and outer world, of the affective tuning of space (Böhme Felt space, p.31, Griffero?). Yet, when the research of the Moreppar project began in 2022, the pandemic situation already was in the process of dissolving. What remained of the pandemic were the spaces it was enmeshed with, still spotted with traces of social distancing instructions and testing facilities, documents keeping records of the pandemic situations and the people who witnessed the pandemic unfold. It quickly became clear that looking at the pandemic through the lens of viral atmospheres necessitated the engagement with the lingering memories of those who were present to experience them first-hand. Memories that would not only recall the pandemic situations but also what lingered on in affective traces of the ways people had been involved in, touched and moved by viral atmospheres. As the ethnographic research of the Moreppar project became focused on what remains of the pandemic, making people’s affective memories a primary focus of the research through qualitative methods in the form of interviews, participatory observation and more, the affective memories of the research’s interlocutors became one of the primary materials of our archive, alongside visual and audio-visual recordings of the spaces that filled and merged with viral pandemic atmospheres and in which people, alongside their memories, lived on.

      Memories cannot provide an account of a time gone by that is congruent with a passed “objective” reality. They are subject to suppression, erosion and forgetting, to distortion. The partiality of subjective human perception and the necessary contraction of expressing feelings—the language of the body—through words, as well as the dynamics of qualitative research, inherently shaped by what interlocutors decide to share and how, as well what and how researchers decide to record, take note of and process, add more layers of complexity to the research on the pandemic atmospheres.

      This website does not want to reconstruct the Viral Atmospheres of the Covid-19 pandemic. Rather, it aims to give an impression of what and how people remember to be moved by, and in doing so is guided by the nexus of pandemic spaces and feelings. These impressions necessarily are fragmental, oscillating and iridescent in nature, leaving our subject of interest, the pandemic atmospheres, in shimmering vagueness.

      About Moreppar

      Mobility Regimes of Pandemic Preparednes and Response: The Case of Covid-19

      The online archive/exhibition “Viral Atmospheres” is a product of the Moreppar research, short for “Mobility Regimes of Pandemic Preparedness: the Case of Covid-19", complementing the forthcoming book that is written in parallel.

      Lingering traces of the pandemic

      When the Moreppar research began in 2022, the acute phase of the pandemic was officially over. Now, in 2025, social distancing signs, still found in unexpected places, have become faint reminders of a time when they were omnipresent in public spaces. Abandoned testing sites are a fading echo of a time when proof of health status was required for work, travel or to protect others; media coverage focuses on other (global) crises that follow one another at breathtaking speed. However, the pandemic has not disappeared entirely from people’s lives. People still fall ill or die; others suffer the long-term effects of infection or vaccination. Staying, too, are the dissatisfaction and anger directed at public health measures; feelings of injustice these experiences provoked often reinforced mistrust of political institutions and scientific knowledge, undermining acceptance of official interventions. Equally enduring are the painful memories of isolation, broken relationships, serious illness, and loss.

      The MoRePPaR project

      The Moreppar project asks: What remains of the pandemic—materially, emotionally, and socially? And how are these remnants shaped by the ruptures the pandemic created in everyday life, particularly the early stages of the outbreak? While some traces are visible—discursive, bureaucratic, spatial, material—we equally focus on the less tangible, but deeply felt, ways the pandemic continues to shape lives: through embodied memories, altered affective relations, and new modes of societal organization. At the heart of the project therefore is an exploration of the affective and emotional consequences of Covid-19: how the crisis affected intimacy, social bonds, biographies, and people’s capacities to survive—biologically, socially, economically.

      Mobility and Control

      One of the defining features of how the pandemic was experienced was the extensive regulation of people’s movement through a variety of models, policies and technologies. During COVID-19, mobility regimes comprised various forms of restriction that often overlapped and were shaped by local, national and global political and legal regulations, as well as the scientific understanding of the impact of mobility on the progression of epidemics. The restriction of movement—as well as the manner in which certain professional groups were permitted or even forced to remain mobile—came to embody state power and its highly unequal operation within and across populations. As governments imposed sweeping restrictions, daily life was upended. Movement across borders and within neighborhoods was curtailed. Work moved to domestic spaces or ceased entirely. In other cases, so-called “essential” workers had to continue working, often without the necessary financial or social recognition. This project argues that the affective and socio-material impact of COVID-19—as well as the entirely novel approaches to restricting and regulating movement—continue to shape people’s lives in ways that surpass how they consciously think about or remember them.

      Research Across Contexts

      What affective, and sometimes traumatic, legacies did these disruptions leave behind? In what ways have pandemic-related health policies reshaped material infrastructures, forms of solidarity, and modes of governance and political engagement? To what extent can these transformations have consequences for broader models of pandemic preparedness? We address these questions through ethnographic research conducted as part of the transnational project, “Mobility Regimes of Pandemic Preparedness and Response: The Case of COVID-19,” funded by the Volkswagen Foundation since 2022. By focusing on four groups that were particularly affected by mobility regulations—cross-border traders in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), essential workers such as truck drivers, market vendors and construction workers in Germany, township residents in South Africa, and young professionals and church members in South Korea—we explore the affective, social and political reverberations of the pandemic in their lives. The project is enriched by additional research in China, Argentina and the USA of involved Master and PhD students.

      Affective Afterlives

      By relating these diverse settings to one another, the project identifies the commonalities and differences in how people coped, or did not cope, with the impact of mobility regimes on the most intimate aspects of their lives, as well as how they are living with the aftermath of the pandemic. A central focus of our analysis is the role of affect, examining how emotions such as fear, exhaustion, grief, hope, and resilience have shaped new modes of solidarity and contestation, and continue to influence people’s attitudes and practices long after the official end of the pandemic. We argue that these affective afterlives, which we conceptualize as “pandemic lingering”, are crucial to understanding the pandemic’s enduring impact on individuals and whole societies, thereby having the potential to influence the trajectory of social coexistence in the years to come.

      Literature

      Our Team

      We are an international team of authors from a variety of institutions, backgrounds and career stages, from the fields of social and cultural anthropology, political science, literature, interdisciplinary art and web development. Over the course of two years, in close connection to the collaborative research process of the Moreppar project, we have worked together on this online archive/exhibiton in various capacities and constellations. We have discussed, researched, sketched, ideated, interviewed, traveled, read, recorded, photographehd, written, imagined, designed, curated, programmed, edited, feedbacked and managed, individually and together, in online meetings and in person.
