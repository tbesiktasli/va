When to move is to live and to live is to move

Research
Paradoxies of State (Un-) Care
Researcher
Simbarashe Nyuke
Date
21/04/2022

‘Ngeskhathi se “Covid-19” sisokolile kakhulu uyabona. Siyahereza thina kodwa ngeskhathi leso kwaba nzima’ During “Covid-19” we suffered a lot, you see. We are ‘hustlers’ (baherezi) but during that time it was difficult. (A young man in his 20’s who stays in Number 4 1 .)

Martha, a woman in her 50’s further explained and demonstrated what it means to move when ‘to move is to live’. I met her at her home in Number 13. When I arrived at her home, she was putting together thin pieces of broken timber, planks, logs, twigs, plastic and paper in between three rocks which are used as a stand for the pot. The entrance to her house was make-shift gate of rusty, corrugated metal sheets and mattress spring wires. The cleaning and bathing water drained from her house through a make-shift gutter to the road. She was wearing a dark old blue jersey, a long maroon dress, and black, torn tennis shoes. In a low frail voice, she explained her experiences during “Covid-19”.

A lot of people lost their jobs. For young mothers after losing a job what(ever) kind of plan you come up with, the police would come and take it. How was one supposed to stay at home without any food? You are washing hands and sanitizing, but you have nothing to eat. You must go out to find vegetables (umroho) in the gardens. We have no electricity, that is why you see granny is making fire right now. I must go fetch wood to make the fire. I can’t cook supper now, I am still busy with other things, I’ll cook it later. “Covid-19”-19 was hard, especially for the young women, because the parcels were not given to everyone. If one was lucky, they got them and if not, that was it. They would tell us to stay at home, so how are you going to fetch firewood? 1 litre of paraffin is very expensive. If I am out to fetch wood, I would cook pap 2 and relish and prepare bathing water for the children, but if I am stuck here, how am I supposed to do that? […] You put on a mask and go out to the bush and dump-sites. It was hard, but you tell yourself God will look out for me when I am in the bushes. As I am telling you that I am going out now to fetch wood, these ones won’t be enough, so it was hard, too much. 1 litre of paraffin is now R22. It’s finished, you must buy more kerosene, with what money are you going to buy it?

With the restriction of movement came the hierarchization of who was allowed to stay mobile. The notion of ‘being essential’ reconfigured social relations and who was entitled to move. Martha, an unemployed mother who moved around the community collecting recyclable items to sell, as well as wood, paper, and plastic for domestic fires, was classified as a non-essential worker. As a result, she was prohibited from leaving her home during the “Covid-19” lockdown. Martha had to keep her family alive through these movements to gather some resources to start a cooking fire, ask for food parcels from neighbours especially when she does not receive any. it was either that or starving to death for her and her family. She chose to move and live. In her own way, she negotiated and exposed the contested definition of essential work, by convincing herself of the necessity of movement to survive. The politicized category of essentiality and the need to move was also very much gendered, with women’s prescribed role as carers pushed to the periphery.

This ‘going to the bushes under God’s eye’ (this expression relates to an expanded interview referenced above) is going out to look for the essentials which enables her to feed her family. Even when meeting the soldiers or police could mean punishment or arrest, Martha had to search. Her gathering and foraging for resources reminded me of the way chickens scratch the earth for food, an aspect of her life which I understood as ukuhereza. Ukuhereza refers to the toughest, least-dignified informal work and labour, necessary to secure a single meal or a two-day supply of food. Ukuhereza is different from hustling, because hustling is slicker, urban, easier and most importantly, hustling takes place in a context of relative abundance. Ukuhereza is not occasional, but rather a mode of survival of the poorest. Ukuhereza is a scratching of the ground when there are no other options, a survival mode peculiar to spaces such as Diepsloot, that resembles holding on by the fingernails, or the Afrikaans word ‘vasbyt’, meaning to hang on, and bite hard.
